The Demographic Cliff

The number of high school graduates is the pool from which traditional college enrollment is drawn. It rose for decades and plateaued near an all-time high. The projected decline after 2025, driven by the drop in births around the 2008 recession, is the enrollment cliff that pressures every tuition-dependent institution.

The long view

Before the decline, the rise. American higher education grew for a century and a half. These two lines are the peak the sector is now contracting from. Under each is what most likely drove every turn: policy, demographics, the economy, war, each sourced, with our inferences labelled.

The finding: the cliff is not in the enrollment numbers yet

We asked the data whether the enrollment cliff is real. The answer is mixed, and honesty requires stating it plainly.

  • Real and observed. Enrollment peaked at 21.0 million in 2010 to 2011 and fell 11.6 percent to 18.6 million by 2022. That decline happened; it is not a forecast.
  • But it ticked back up to 19.0 million in 2023, and the NCES national enrollment projection runs modestly upward through 2031, not down. NCES does not forecast a continuing cliff in total enrollment.
  • The cliff is a demographic argument, not an enrollment one. It rests on US births peaking in 2007 and falling about 17 percent, so fewer 18-year-olds arrive starting around 2025. That is the pipeline tracked in the high-school-graduate charts below, a leading indicator, not the enrollment count itself.

So the enrollment line above stops at the last actual year and draws no projection. Drawing a plunging line into the future would be a fabrication, because NCES projects the opposite. The defensible reading: enrollment already fell once, and the demographic pipeline is shrinking, so the risk ahead is demographic. It is not that NCES says enrollment will crater, because it does not.
